Overview

What is Pumping Stations?

A pumping station is a packaged or built-up arrangement of pumps, piping, valves, controls and tanks that moves water from where it is stored to where it is needed. In buildings it supplies domestic water to upper floors, transfers water between sumps and overhead tanks, and boosts pressure where mains pressure is not enough.

The key design numbers are flow and head. Flow comes from the peak water demand of the building, based on occupancy and fixtures. Head is the total pressure the pump must develop: the static lift, the pressure needed at the farthest outlet, and the friction losses in pipes, valves and fittings. The pump is then chosen so its curve meets the system curve near its best efficiency point.

Because water supply is continuous, pumping stations are designed for reliability: duty and standby pumps, automatic changeover, protection against dry running, and layouts that allow maintenance without stopping supply.

Why It Matters

What goes wrong without it

Pressure swings

Poorly controlled pumps cause low pressure on top floors and high pressure below.

Pump failure

A single pump with no standby leaves the building without water.

Energy waste

Oversized pumps throttled by valves burn power.

Noise and vibration

Pumps on rigid mounts transmit noise through the structure.

Technical Details

Parameters, standards and practice

  • Selection near the best efficiency point of the pump curve, avoiding operation far to either side.
  • Net Positive Suction Head available must exceed what the pump needs, with a safety margin, to prevent cavitation.
  • Suction pipe velocity kept low, typically about 1 to 1.5 m/s, and discharge velocity moderate, up to about 2.5 to 3 m/s.
  • Duty and standby arrangement, such as 1+1 or 2+1, with automatic alternation.
  • VFD control keeps a constant pressure and cuts energy at partial demand.
  • Pressure vessel sized to limit starts and absorb surges.
  • Dry-run, over-current and phase-failure protection.
  • Vibration isolators and flexible connectors to cut noise.
  • Pumps, motors and valves to applicable IS or ISO standards.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a booster pump and a transfer pump?

A transfer pump moves water between tanks. A booster pump raises pressure in the supply line to the fixtures.

Why use a VFD?

It varies pump speed to hold constant pressure, which saves energy and reduces wear compared with throttling.

What is cavitation?

Vapour bubbles forming at the pump inlet because suction pressure is too low. It causes noise and damage.

How many standby pumps do I need?

At least one, for critical supplies. The number depends on how essential continuous supply is.
